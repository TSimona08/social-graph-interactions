Week 4 · De Rebus Philosophorum

What Weight
Reveals

1,374 philosophers, 9,139 cross-references, one algorithm that has never read a word any of them wrote — and it still gets their history right.

Meet the Cast

Who You'll Meet Below

Seven names that come back later, in a network that knows nothing about any of them except who links to whom.

Plato c. 428 – 348 BC · Athens

Student of Socrates. Founded Western philosophy's oldest argument: that the world we see is a shadow of a truer, unchanging one.

Aristotle 384 – 322 BC · Athens/Macedon

Plato's student, then his sharpest critic. Tutored Alexander the Great before founding his own school.

Avicenna c. 980 – 1037 · Persia

Physician and philosopher who fused Aristotle with Islamic theology. Part of a tight circle with Al-Farabi and Averroes.

John Locke 1632 – 1704 · England

Blank-slate mind, government by consent. Read closely by the American Founders a century later.

Immanuel Kant 1724 – 1804 · Prussia

Rewrote the rules for what philosophy can even ask: what we can know, and how we ought to act.

Karl Marx 1818 – 1883 · Germany/England

Critiqued capitalism so thoroughly it split a century of politics in two. Almost always co-credited with Friedrich Engels.

From people to dots Every philosopher here has a Wikipedia article; every link between two articles becomes a tie in the network below. 1,374 philosophers, 9,139 ties — too tangled to read by eye. The rest of this post is an algorithm finding the structure in that tangle, using nothing but who links to whom.

The Movers

Who Changes, and Why

Ranked by the single strongest tie that pulled them across the boundary. The pattern isn't random: it's mostly pairs and small circles, tied together by an unusually heavy, repeated cross-reference.

Loading…

The Islamic Golden Age, reunited Avicenna, from the cast above, moves together with Al-Farabi, Averroes, Al-Ghazali, Ibn al-Haytham, and Mulla Sadra into one weighted community. Unweighted, they were split apart and outnumbered by the larger European cluster they were nominally part of. Their heavy internal cross-referencing is enough to pull them back together once it's counted.
Some moves are just... intense friendships Friedrich Hayek and Ludwig von Mises (weight 12), Roger Bacon and Robert Grosseteste (weight 11), Thomas More and Erasmus (weight 24, the single heaviest tie in the whole network) — these are pairs whose Wikipedia articles reference each other so often that weighting alone is enough to override the coarser unweighted structure and drag both members somewhere new, together.
The Sanity Check

Plato and Aristotle Don't Move

Teacher and student, portraits 1 and 2 above — and the most famous pair in the dataset lands in the same community in both partitions. Whatever else weighting changes, it isn't reshuffling the network at random.

0.66 NMI, not 1.0 and not 0 High enough that the two partitions are clearly describing the same underlying network — low enough that weight is doing real work, not just noise.
17.2% Moved, not "most" The big blocks mostly hold. It's the boundary cases — philosophers with one unusually strong tie outside their era’s dominant cluster — that flip.

Aristotle's tradition, per the network: it's Plato Aristotle belongs to 20 distinctive link communities (giant cluster excluded) — and Plato is in every single one. The network never finds a standalone "Aristotelian" circle; it finds the two of them as one inseparable pair.

John Locke has the most distinctive circles of any leader (28): he sits at the hinge between British Empiricism and the Enlightenment thinkers who read him.
How it's built 1444 philosophers born before 1900, English Wikipedia (frozen snapshot, 2026‑09‑15), directed links with repeat-count weights, summed into an undirected graph two ways: binary (linked or not) and weighted (summed link count). Communities via nx.community.louvain_communities (weight=None vs weight="weight", same seed), restricted to the 1374-node giant component. NMI via sklearn.metrics.normalized_mutual_info_score. Community leaders: nx.betweenness_centrality computed on each community's own induced subgraph. Link communities: hand-implemented Ahn–Bagrow–Lehmann algorithm — weighted-Jaccard similarity between edges sharing a node, single-linkage clustered via scipy.cluster.hierarchy.linkage, cut at the threshold maximizing partition density. Layout: spring layout on the weighted graph, held fixed across both views so only the coloring changes. Code: analysis/week4/.
